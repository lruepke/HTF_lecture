Exercise 1

Transient flow in a lid-driven cavity

In this section, we explore how the fluid flow in a lid-driven cavity develops over time, and how the behavior and time scales of the simulation are influenced by model parameters. Understanding this helps to interpret simulation results and perform meaningful parametric studies.

Time scales and regimes of viscous flow

Reynolds number

One important dimensionless number in fluid dynamics is the Reynolds number. It is defined as:

(5)\[Re = \frac{UL}{\nu}\]

where:

  • \(U\) is the characteristic velocity (e.g., lid speed),

  • \(L\) is the characteristic length scale of the domain (e.g., cavity height or width),

  • \(\nu\) is the kinematic viscosity.

The Reynolds number compares inertia (moving fluid tends to keep moving) with viscosity (friction within the fluid, which slows the motion down):

  • Small Re: viscosity wins. The flow is smooth and predictable, and the fluid moves in orderly layers. This is called laminar flow.

  • Large Re: inertia wins. The flow becomes more complex, with additional vortices and eventually unsteady flow, and at very large Re it becomes turbulent (chaotic).

The values of Re at which these changes happen depend on the geometry of the problem, so there is no single threshold that applies to all flows.

Why this matters for hydrothermal flow

Later in the course we model fluid flow through porous rocks. There, the pores are tiny and the flow is very slow, so the Reynolds number is much smaller than 1. Viscosity wins completely, and the flow can be described by the much simpler Darcy’s law instead of the full Navier-Stokes equations.

We are defining a 3-D mesh with a width and height of 0.1 m and a thickness of 0.01 m. A potential pitfall is line 16, where a global scaling of the spatial dimensions is introduced. The 3rd dimension does not matter here, as we are running the simulation in 2d mode (boundary faces are set to empty). The lid velocity is set in 0/U to 1 m/s. The kinematic viscosity is set in constant/physicalProperties to 0.01 m^2/s. The numerical resolution is set in system/blockMeshDict to 20 cells in the x and y direction, which gives us a grid spacing of 0.005 m.

The Reynolds number for this case is:

(6)\[Re = \frac{UL}{\nu} = \frac{1~\text{m/s} \cdot 0.1~\text{m}}{0.01~\text{m}^2/\text{s}} = 10\]

This is a small Reynolds number: viscosity dominates, and we expect a smooth, laminar flow with a single large vortex.

The range is enormous. The mantle is so viscous that inertia plays no role at all: if a plate stopped moving, the mantle flow below it would stop practically at once. This is why mantle convection is modeled with the Stokes equations, which are the Navier-Stokes equations without the inertia terms. The flow through the pores of a rock, which is what this course is about, is again dominated by viscosity (see the box “Why this matters for hydrothermal flow” above).

Time scales

Let’s check the time scales of the problem. One time scale of interest is the convection time scale, which is given by:

(7)\[t_\text{conv} \sim \frac{L}{U}\]

where:

  • \(L\) is the characteristic length scale of the domain (e.g., cavity height or length),

  • \(U\) is the characteristic velocity (e.g., lid speed).

This relation tells us how long it takes for a fluid parcel to travel the length of the domain. For our case, this is:

(8)\[t_\text{conv} \sim \frac{0.1~\text{m}}{1~\text{m/s}} = 0.1~\text{s}\]

Another important time scale is the viscous diffusion time scale, which is given by:

(9)\[t_\text{visc} \sim \frac{L^2}{\nu}\]

This relation tells us how quickly the motion of the lid spreads into the fluid. Higher viscosity or smaller domains lead to faster development of flow patterns. For our case, this is:

(10)\[t_\text{visc} \sim \frac{(0.1~\text{m})^2}{0.01~\text{m}^2/\text{s}} = 1~\text{s}\]

You know this from heat conduction

The time it takes heat to diffuse over a distance \(L\) is \(t \sim L^2/\kappa\), with the thermal diffusivity \(\kappa\). This is how we estimate how long a dike or the oceanic lithosphere needs to cool. Viscosity works the same way: the kinematic viscosity \(\nu\) is the “diffusivity of momentum”, and it has the same units as \(\kappa\) (m²/s). The fluid in the cavity starts at rest, and the motion of the lid diffuses into it just like heat diffuses into cold rock.

So \(t_\text{visc}\) is our estimate for how long it takes until the whole cavity is moving and the flow is steady. Such estimates tell you whether something takes milliseconds, seconds or hours. They are not exact: expect them to be off by a factor of ten or more. How good is the estimate here? Our base case runs until 0.5 s and writes results every 0.1 s. You will measure the time to steady state yourself in the first task below.

Courant number and numerical stability

Finally, we need to check the Courant number. It tells us whether our time step is small enough for the mesh we use (we will get to the details later). The Courant number is defined as:

(11)\[Co = \frac{U \Delta t}{\Delta x}\]

where:

  • \(U\) is the characteristic velocity (e.g., lid speed),

  • \(\Delta t\) is the time step size,

  • \(\Delta x\) is the grid spacing (e.g., cell size).

The Courant number tells us how many cells the fluid moves in one time step. The rule of thumb is: in one time step, the fluid should not move further than one cell, so \(Co\) should stay below 1. If it gets larger, the results become less accurate, and the simulation can become unstable: the values start to oscillate and grow until they are meaningless. For our case, the Courant number is:

(12)\[Co = \frac{1~\text{m/s} \cdot 0.005~\text{s}}{0.005~\text{m}} = 1\]

This is just about acceptable. If we increase the lid speed or make the cells smaller, we have to make the time step smaller as well.

You do not have to calculate the Courant number by hand: the solver reports it in every time step. If you run the case with ./run.sh, look into the log file:

grep "Courant" log.icoFoam | tail -1

Courant Number mean: 0.222158 max: 0.852134

The maximum is a bit smaller than our estimate, because the fluid inside the cavity moves slower than the lid. Check this line whenever you change the velocity, the mesh, or the time step.

Exploration Framework

Let’s modify the base case and explore how the flow develops under different physical and numerical conditions. Most of the tasks below compare several runs, e.g. with different viscosities. Here is how to set this up.

Comparing runs side by side

One folder per run. Never change a case that you want to compare with. Make a copy for every variation and give it a name that says what is different, e.g. for a smaller viscosity:

cd $HOME/HydrothermalFoam_runs
cp -r cavity2D cavity2D_nu0.001
cd cavity2D_nu0.001

Change the parameter, e.g. nu in constant/physicalProperties, and run the case with ./run.sh. The script cleans the copied results first.

Results next to each other in ParaView.

  1. Open the a.foam files of both cases (File → Open, once for each case folder) and click Apply. Both cases now lie on top of each other.

  2. Select the second case in the Pipeline Browser and apply Filters → Alphabetical → Transform. In its properties, set Translate to 0.12 0 0 and click Apply. This moves the second case 0.12 m to the right, next to the first one. For a third case, use 0.24 0 0, and so on.

  3. If the original second case is still visible, hide it with the eye icon in the Pipeline Browser, so that only the moved copy is shown.

  4. Color both by the same field and give them the same color range (Rescale to Custom Range). Otherwise, the same color means different values in the two cases.

Further filters, e.g. a Calculator or Surface LIC, are applied to the transformed case. To compare profiles, apply Plot Over Line to the original cases, not to the moved one, so that you can use the same line for both. Show both lines in the same Line Chart View: then they are directly on top of each other.

  1. Baseline Case: predict, then measure

    • Use the standard lid-driven cavity example with:

      • Domain size: \(L = 0.1~\text{m}\)

      • Viscosity: \(\nu = 0.01~\text{m}^2/\text{s}\)

      • Lid speed: \(U = 1~\text{m/s}\)

    • Predict first: before you run anything, write down when you expect the flow to be steady, based on the time scales above.

    • Measure: set writeInterval 2; in system/controlDict, so that results are written every 0.01 s, and run the case again. In ParaView, select a.foam and apply Filters → Data Analysis → Probe Location with the point in the center of the cavity, (0.05 0.05 0.005). Then apply Filters → Data Analysis → Plot Data Over Time to the probe and plot the velocity magnitude against time.

    • Questions to explore:

      • After how much time does the velocity in the center stop changing? Compare this with your prediction. By what factor were you off, and why can a scaling estimate be off by that much?

      • Look at the time steps written by the original case (every 0.1 s). Why did the original case look so “boring”?

      • What is the shape of the vortex, and where is its center?

      • How does the velocity field look like?

  2. Vary Viscosity

    • Change the viscosity in constant/physicalProperties from \(\nu = 0.01~\text{m}^2/\text{s}\) to \(0.1~\text{m}^2/\text{s}\) and to \(0.001~\text{m}^2/\text{s}\). Use a copy of the case for each value.

    • Before you run: calculate \(Re\) and \(t_\text{visc}\) for each value. Does the run time of 0.5 s still make sense? For \(\nu = 0.001~\text{m}^2/\text{s}\), increase endTime in system/controlDict (e.g. to 2 s).

    • Questions to explore:

      • How fast does the vortex form? Measure it as in task 1 and compare with your estimate. Is the trend right?

      • How does the flow pattern change? Where is the center of the vortex?

  3. Change Lid Velocity

    • Keep \(\nu\) fixed and vary lid velocity \(U\).

    • Questions to explore:

      • Does a higher velocity lead to a longer or shorter transient?

      • Does the vortex strength or location change?

  4. Mesh Sensitivity

    • Increase mesh resolution (e.g., from 20x20 to 40x40).

    • Observe differences in vortex shape and location.

    • Discuss numerical vs physical convergence.

  5. A deep cavity: how many vortices?

    • Make a copy of the base case and make the cavity four times as deep as it is wide (0.1 m wide, 0.4 m deep). In system/blockMeshDict, change the y coordinates of the upper vertices. Which ones are they? Also change the number of cells in the y direction, so that the cells stay square.

    • Set endTime 1; in system/controlDict.

    • Predict first: before you run the case, sketch the flow you expect on a piece of paper. How many vortices will there be, and in which direction do they rotate?

    • Run the case and look at it in ParaView:

      • Color it by the velocity magnitude. What do you see?

      • Now switch to a logarithmic color scale: in the Color Map Editor, tick Use Log Scale When Mapping Data To Colors (click on the gear icon if you do not see this option), and set the range to 1e-7 to 1 with Rescale to Custom Range. What do you see now?

      • Make the flow paths visible with Surface LIC, a display mode that draws fine streaks along the flow direction:

        1. Apply a Calculator with the result name direction and the expression U/mag(U). This is the flow direction, with all vectors of the same length. Without it, the slow vortices would not show up.

        2. Apply a second Calculator to the first one, with the result name log_speed and the expression log10(mag(U)).

        3. In the toolbar, change the display mode from Surface to Surface LIC. In the Properties panel, set Vectors to direction.

        4. Color by log_speed and set the range to -6 to 0 with Rescale to Custom Range. The color now shows the speed: -1 means 0.1 m/s, -6 means 0.000001 m/s.

    • Questions to explore:

      • How many vortices are there? Was your sketch right?

      • The streaks show the paths of the fluid, but not in which direction it moves. Think: the lid moves to the right, so in which direction does the top vortex rotate? Where two vortices touch, the fluid of both moves in the same direction, like two gears. So in which direction do the middle and the bottom vortex rotate?

      • Check your answer: use Plot Over Line along the vertical center line, from (0.05 0 0.005) to (0.05 0.4 0.005), and look at the horizontal velocity U_X in the SpreadSheet View. Positive values mean flow to the right, negative values flow to the left. How much weaker is each vortex than the one above it?

      • What does this mean for the fluid at the bottom of a deep, narrow cavity, e.g. a deep fissure below a flowing current?

      • Optional: try a cavity that is two or three times as deep as it is wide. How does the number of vortices depend on the depth?

Things to keep in mind

  • You may have to adjust the time step size to maintain a stable Courant number.

  • When changing time step size, make sure to adjust the output interval accordingly.

  • Make a copy of the case for each variation, and show the results next to each other in ParaView (see “Comparing runs side by side” above).

  • Use the “plot over line” filter in ParaView to extract velocity profiles and compare different cases

  • Use the “stream tracer” filter in ParaView to visualize the flow field and vortex structure.

Expected Observations

  • Baseline: the velocity in the center reaches 99 % of its final value after about 0.07 s, more than ten times faster than the estimate \(t_\text{visc} \sim 1\) s. So the original case was already steady at its first output time (0.1 s). The estimate gets the trend right (twice the viscosity, twice as fast), but not the exact number. See the box below for why.

  • High viscosity: Flow develops quickly and becomes smooth and laminar.

  • Low viscosity: Transient lasts longer, possible oscillations or instabilities.

  • High lid velocity: Stronger vortices, higher Reynolds number, more complex flow.

  • Deep cavity (four times as deep as wide): three vortices on top of each other, with centers at about 0.38, 0.24 and 0.09 m height, i.e. about 1.4 cavity widths apart. Neighboring vortices rotate in opposite directions, like gears. Each vortex is about 350 times weaker than the one above it: the largest horizontal velocities on the center line are about 0.19, 5e-4 and 1e-6 m/s. With a linear color scale, only the top vortex is visible; with Surface LIC on the direction field, all three show up as closed loops. Seen from the front, the top vortex rotates clockwise, the middle one counterclockwise, and the bottom one clockwise again. The fluid at the bottom is practically at rest. These stacked vortices are known as Moffatt eddies, after the fluid dynamicist Keith Moffatt, who described them in 1964.

  • Fine mesh: Captures finer details (e.g., secondary vortices), more accurate but slower to compute.

Why is the flow steady after 0.07 s and not after 1 s?

The estimate \(t_\text{visc} \sim L^2/\nu\) only tells us the order of magnitude. Two things make the real time shorter:

  1. The motion of the lid does not have to diffuse across the whole cavity. The flow pattern is set up once the motion has reached about the center of the cavity, which is only half the distance, \(L/2\). Because the distance enters squared, half the distance needs only a quarter of the time.

  2. The distance over which something diffuses in the time \(t\) is about \(2\sqrt{\nu t}\), not \(\sqrt{\nu t}\). You know this from the cooling of the oceanic lithosphere, whose thickness grows as about \(2.3\sqrt{\kappa t}\).

Together, the motion reaches the center when \(2\sqrt{\nu t} \approx L/2\), i.e. after

\[t \approx \frac{L^2}{16\,\nu} = \frac{(0.1~\text{m})^2}{16 \cdot 0.01~\text{m}^2/\text{s}} \approx 0.06~\text{s},\]

close to what we measured. The dependence on \(\nu\) stays the same: with a ten times smaller viscosity, \(\nu = 0.001~\text{m}^2/\text{s}\), the flow needs about 0.7 s, ten times longer. The lesson: scaling estimates are great for trends and for comparing cases, but numerical factors like \(1/16\) can make a big difference in absolute numbers.
